# What the paper establishes

Riccardo Panza's idea gives a clear name and picture to a particular kind of record prime gap.

Imagine each odd prime as a centred row of blocks. Moving to the next prime adds the same number of blocks on each side. The earliest records for these additions are 1, 2, 3, 4 and then 7. That last jump skips 5 and 6. It occurs between 113 and 127 and is the first **prime-gap rupture**.

The skipped sizes later appear: step 5 at 139→149 and step 6 at 199→211. The paper calls those first appearances recovery. This does not mean that the primes are correcting themselves.

The original thirteen rupture examples are numerically correct. The expanded audit supports **68 ruptures within the published complete-search boundary of 10²⁰**, with all skipped sizes recovered for the first **66**. The last two remain partly unresolved. A further listed event is included as a candidate beyond that boundary.

The longest completed recovery takes **eight subsequent ruptures**, reached by events 27 and 59. That is the largest delay observed, not a universal limit. The original thirteen had a maximum of three, showing why larger checks matter.

The thirteen composite numbers between 113 and 127 form the first rupture desert. Their factorisations supply the complementary picture of a prime-factor oasis. Whether such intervals have unusually rich factor structure remains a question for Paper Two.

The useful contribution is the combination of a precise classification, an accessible visual representation and reproducible recovery measurements. It does not predict primes or solve an established conjecture. Half-gap sequences and record-gap differences already exist in the literature and are credited.
